## Abstract

A person who cannot do a task can still invoke a machine system many times, and the claim that power therefore equals compute holds only if they can tell good outputs from bad. This paper models an invoker who resamples a system with per-attempt success probability $p$ and accepts outputs with a check of sensitivity $s$ and false-acceptance rate $f$. Accepting the first output that passes converges to the check's precision, $sp/[sp+f(1-p)]$, a known limit for verifier-based selection. A target reliability is reachable with finite compute exactly when $s/f$ exceeds the target's odds divided by the generator's odds. Across tasks, resampling falls short of a check's pooled precision by the covariance between per-task acceptance and precision, divided by mean acceptance; with a common check the ceiling lies between single-attempt accuracy and the pooled formula. Re-checking a candidate changes nothing when verdicts are fixed by the candidate. On 40 public olympiad problems a small current model was right on 88% of attempts. Its own verdicts accepted 41% of wrong attempts, mostly its repeated errors, and capped resampling at 92%, against 94% with a stronger model as checker and 95% with a perfect check. On 25 harder problems (58% right), its self-check raised reliability only to 60%, while the stronger checker reached the 72% a perfect check allows. By hand classification, at least 30% and 67% of the stronger model's verdicts invoked a remembered answer, so its advantage partly reflects memory. With these samples most differences between checks remain unresolved.

## 1. Buying reliability without the skill to check it

Suppose someone needs the answer to a competition mathematics problem they cannot solve, and a language model answers problems of that kind correctly most of the time. Asking again costs little. In the measurement reported below, a small current model answered 40 olympiad-level problems correctly on 88% of attempts. With 6 attempts per problem and a perfect way of recognising a correct answer, reliability rose to 95%. When the model judged its own answers, reliability reached 92% within 3 attempts, and extrapolating from the observed attempts gives no gain from larger budgets.

A ruler who wants a garment need not learn to sew; he commands people who sew. The claim that externalised intelligence makes everyone such a ruler, so that what a person can bring about depends on the computation they command more than on the skills they possess, can be summarised as "power equals compute". Delegation is old. What is new is a delegate whose attempts are cheap to repeat, which makes repetition look like a substitute for skill. Repetition helps, however, only if the invoker can tell good outputs from bad ones, and telling them apart is itself a competence. This paper calls the condition in which attaining an outcome no longer requires the invoker to possess the competence to produce it *post-competence*, and asks when compute delivers it. The term has been used in educational linguistics for a different idea, an orientation of language education beyond the competence model [@anderson2023].

The paper models an invoker who draws attempts from a system with per-attempt success probability $p$ and accepts outputs with a check of sensitivity $s$ and false-acceptance rate $f$. The check may be the invoker's own judgment, a heuristic, another model or a test. Accepting the first output that passes the check converges, as attempts grow, to $sp/[sp + f(1-p)]$, the check's precision at the generator's base rate; call it the precision ceiling. Compute buys reliability up to this ceiling and no further.

The single-task formula is prior work. Davis and colleagues derive it for a "verifier-based judge" and show that resampling gains over one attempt exactly when the check accepts correct outputs more often than incorrect ones [@davis2024]. Stroebl, Kapoor and Narayanan show that unit-test verifiers with false positives cap the accuracy of resampling on code benchmarks, so that a weaker model cannot reach a stronger model's single-attempt accuracy this way [@stroebl2024]. Related work measures how the share of problems solved by some sample grows with the number of samples [@brown2024; @schaeffer2025], how majority voting can degrade as calls are added [@chen2024calls], and how to allocate test-time compute across prompts [@snell2024]. Verifiers trained to rank sampled solutions go back at least to work on grade-school mathematics [@cobbe2021; @lightman2023]. The broader problem of supervising a system that may outperform its supervisor is studied as scalable oversight. People assisted by an unreliable dialogue model outperformed both the model and their own unaided performance on two question-answering tasks [@bowman2022]. Model-written critiques helped people find flaws they would otherwise have missed [@saunders2022]. Strong models fine-tuned on a weak model's labels outperformed their supervisor without recovering their full capability [@burns2023]. These studies ask whether weak supervision can be made better; this paper asks how far a given weak check lets compute go.

Beyond these results, the paper does the following. It gives the number of attempts needed to reach a target reliability, finite exactly when the target lies below the ceiling, and restates that condition as a minimum likelihood ratio $s/f$ that the invoker's check must have. For a population of tasks, it proves that the reliability reached by resampling falls short of the check's pooled precision by the covariance between per-task acceptance rate and per-task precision, divided by the mean acceptance rate. It also proves that with a common check the population ceiling lies between single-attempt accuracy and the pooled formula, reaching the former when every task is always or never solved, and that spreading task difficulty around a fixed mean can only lower it. It gives the ceiling under repeated checking of the same candidate, which is unchanged when verdicts are fixed by the candidate and, with independent verdicts, concentrates on the answers the check accepts most often. And it measures these quantities with a current model on a main set and a harder robustness set of public competition problems, including checks available to someone who cannot solve the problems.

## 2. Attainable sets, performance gains and novice sufficiency

Fix a class $\mathcal X$ of tasks, each with a stated acceptance standard. For a person $i$, let $\Pi_i$ be the strategies they can actually invoke given the systems, tools, money and permissions available to them, and let a strategy $\pi$ have compute cost $C(\pi)$ and probability $p_x(\pi)$ of meeting the standard on task $x$. The attainable set at budget $c$ and reliability $\rho$ is $$\mathcal A_i(c,\rho)=\{x\in\mathcal X:\ \exists\,\pi\in\Pi_i,\ C(\pi)\le c,\ p_x(\pi)\ge\rho\}.$$ It plays the role of Sen's capability set, the set of functionings a person is able to achieve [@sen1985], restricted to outcomes with stated standards, budgets and reliability. The compute threshold of task $x$ is $c^*_x(\rho)=\inf\{c: x\in\mathcal A_i(c,\rho)\}$, infinite when no budget suffices. Read literally, "power equals compute" says that $c^*_x(\rho)$ is finite for the tasks a person cares about and does not depend on the person's own competence.

Claims of performance improvement and of novice sufficiency should be kept apart. *Performance improvement* means that assistance raises output, quality or speed relative to working without it. *Novice sufficiency* means that a person without the relevant training reaches the standard $\rho$ on $x$ with assistance, so that $x$ enters their attainable set. Improvement can occur without sufficiency, and sufficiency can hold while experts remain better.

The evidence on improvement is substantial. In a preregistered experiment with 453 college-educated professionals, access to ChatGPT cut the time spent on writing tasks by 40% and raised assessed quality by 18%, and inequality between workers decreased [@noy2023]. Among 5,172 customer-support agents, a conversational assistant raised issues resolved per hour by 15% on average, with less experienced and lower-skilled workers improving in both speed and quality [@brynjolfsson2025]. These are gains for people already doing the work.

Evidence about reliability under assistance is less favourable. In a randomised trial in which 16 experienced open-source developers completed 246 tasks, allowing AI tools increased completion time by 19%, although the developers had forecast a 24% reduction and estimated a 20% reduction afterwards [@becker2025]. They accepted under 44% of AI generations and spent about 9% of their AI-assisted time reviewing and cleaning AI output [@becker2025, app. C.1.4]. In a field experiment with 758 consultants, AI access improved work on tasks inside the frontier of the system's capability but made consultants less likely to reach a correct answer on a task selected to lie outside it [@dellacqua2026]. The first study shows verification consuming expert time; the second shows assisted people accepting wrong output. Studies of automation describe the same difficulty for human monitors. Automating a process leaves the operator to monitor a system that was installed because it does the job better than they can [@bainbridge1983, sec. 1.1.3]. With imperfect decision aids, automation bias produces errors of omission and commission in naive and expert users alike and is not prevented by training or instructions [@parasuraman2010]. Measured capability also depends on the reliability demanded: for frontier agents on software tasks, the task length completed with 80% success is 4 to 6 times shorter than the length completed with 50% success [@kwa2025, sec. 3.2.1].

Novice sufficiency is therefore a statement about reliability under the invoker's own acceptance decision. The model below asks how much of that reliability compute can buy for someone who cannot do the task and has only an imperfect way of checking it.

## 3. A model of repeated invocation

Fix one task. The invoker can draw attempts $Y_1,Y_2,\dots$ from a system. Given the task, attempts are independent and identically distributed, which holds exactly for independent samples from a fixed model and prompt. Let $p$ be the probability that an attempt is correct. The invoker applies a check to each attempt and obtains a verdict $V_j\in\{0,1\}$. Given the attempts, verdicts are independent, with sensitivity $s=\Pr(V=1\mid\text{correct})$ and false-acceptance rate $f=\Pr(V=1\mid\text{incorrect})$. In the vocabulary of signal detection, $s$ is a hit rate and $f$ a false-alarm rate [@green1966]. An attempt is accepted with probability $a=sp+f(1-p)$.

With a budget of $n$ attempts the invoker can use one of the following protocols.

- *Accept first*: return $Y_1$. Reliability is $p$ for every $n$.
- *Plurality vote*: return the most frequent answer among $Y_1,\dots,Y_n$, breaking ties uniformly at random.
- *Accept first passing*: return the first attempt whose verdict is $1$; if none passes, return $Y_1$. In the abstaining variant nothing is returned in that case.
- *Oracle check*: accept first passing with $s=1$ and $f=0$.

Compute is counted in generations. Under a model check each attempt costs one generation and one verdict call, and a verdict on a candidate already judged can be reused. A check that needs further generations, such as asking again and comparing answers, spends part of the same budget, so its curves are indexed by generations as well. Section 5 reports tokens and prices.

## 4. What resampling can buy

### 4.1 One task

**Proposition 1.** Let $a>0$. Under accept first passing, $$R(n)=\pi-(\pi-p)(1-a)^{n-1},\qquad \pi=\frac{sp}{sp+f(1-p)}.$$ Hence $R(1)=p$, $R(n)$ increases strictly with $n$ when $s>f$, $0<p<1$ and $a<1$, and $R(n)\to\pi$. In odds, $\pi/(1-\pi)=(s/f)\cdot p/(1-p)$. The abstaining variant has $R(n)=\pi[1-(1-a)^n]$ and the same limit.

*Proof.* Because attempts are independent and identically distributed, the first accepted attempt is correct with probability $sp/a=\pi$ whatever its position, and some attempt among the first $n$ is accepted with probability $1-(1-a)^n$. If none is accepted, $Y_1$ is a rejected attempt and is correct with probability $(1-s)p/(1-a)$. Hence $R(n)=\pi[1-(1-a)^n]+(1-a)^{n-1}(1-s)p$. Since $\pi(1-a)-(1-s)p=\pi-p=p(s-a)/a$, this equals the stated form. Because $s-a=(1-p)(s-f)$ is positive when $s>f$ and $p<1$, the term $(\pi-p)(1-a)^{n-1}$ is positive and shrinks with $n$. $\square$

Davis and colleagues obtain the same limit for a judge that returns a random answer when every candidate is rejected [@davis2024, thm. 1–2]. The limit is the positive predictive value of the check at base rate $p$. Its dependence on the base rate is the point Meehl and Rosen made about psychometric signs [@meehl1955]: a check that separates well can still pass many errors when correct outputs are rare. The odds form shows that the invoker's evidence is applied once. Every accepted output has passed one check, and no amount of resampling raises its odds above the generator's odds multiplied by the check's likelihood ratio.

**Proposition 2.** Let $0<p<1$, $f>0$ and $a<1$. For a target $p<\rho<\pi$, the smallest budget with $R(n)\ge\rho$ is $$n^*(\rho)=1+\left\lceil\frac{\ln[(\pi-\rho)/(\pi-p)]}{\ln(1-a)}\right\rceil,$$ and $n^*(\rho)=\infty$ for $\rho\ge\pi$. A target $\rho\in(p,1)$ is attainable with finite compute if and only if $$\frac{s}{f}>\frac{\rho/(1-\rho)}{p/(1-p)}.$$ As $\rho\uparrow\pi$, $n^*(\rho)$ grows like $\ln[1/(\pi-\rho)]/\lvert\ln(1-a)\rvert$.

*Proof.* Rearrange $(\pi-p)(1-a)^{n-1}\le\pi-\rho$, dividing by $\ln(1-a)<0$. The second statement is $\pi>\rho$ written in odds. $\square$

In the example of Figure 1a and 1d, $p=0.4$, $s=0.9$ and $f=0.15$. The ceiling is 0.8, the check's likelihood ratio is 6, and a reliability of 0.75 takes 5 attempts while 0.79 takes 8; 0.8 is never reached. An oracle check reaches 0.99 in 10 attempts. At this $p$, a check needs a likelihood ratio above 13.5 to make 0.9 attainable and above 148.5 to make 0.99 attainable (Figure 1b). Proposition 2 is the compute threshold of Section 2 for a single strategy: the task enters the attainable set at finite cost only if the check is good enough, and otherwise extra compute moves reliability towards the ceiling but never to the target.

Plurality voting needs no check. By Condorcet's jury theorem and its generalisation to plurality choice among several options, the vote of independent attempts converges to the correct answer when it is the most probable answer, and to a wrong answer when some wrong answer is more probable [@condorcet1785; @list2001]. The vote therefore replaces the invoker's verification competence with an assumption about how errors are spread: one systematic wrong answer that is more frequent than the right one defeats it (Figure 1a). Correlation among voters weakens the classical guarantee [@boland1989; @ladha1992], and when tasks differ in difficulty, vote accuracy can fall as calls are added [@chen2024calls]; self-consistency decoding applies the same vote to sampled reasoning paths [@wang2022]. Building reliable systems from unreliable parts by redundancy and majority goes back to von Neumann and to Moore and Shannon [@vonneumann1956; @moore1956], and it requires the independence that systematic errors violate.

### 4.2 Populations of tasks

A user rarely faces a single task. Let tasks be drawn from a population, with task-level parameters $p_x$, $s_x$, $f_x$, acceptance rate $a_x$ and ceiling $\pi_x$. Population reliability is the average task-level reliability, and under the fallback rule it converges to $\mathbb E[\pi_x\mathbf 1\{a_x>0\}+p_x\mathbf 1\{a_x=0\}]$.

**Proposition 3.** (a) Let $P_{\text{pool}}=\mathbb E[a_x\pi_x]/\mathbb E[a_x]$ be the pooled precision, the share of correct outputs among accepted ones when each task is attempted once. For the abstaining protocol, with $\pi_x=0$ when $a_x=0$, $$P_{\text{pool}}-\lim_{n\to\infty}R(n)=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(a_x,\pi_x)}{\mathbb E[a_x]}.$$ (b) If $s_x=s$ and $f_x=f$ for all tasks, with $s>f>0$, then $$\bar p\le\mathbb E[\pi(p_x)]\le\pi(\bar p)=P_{\text{pool}},\qquad \bar p=\mathbb E[p_x],$$ with equality on the right if and only if $p_x$ is constant and on the left if and only if $p_x\in\{0,1\}$ almost surely. To second order, $$\mathbb E[\pi(p_x)]\approx\pi(\bar p)-\frac{sf(s-f)}{[f+(s-f)\bar p]^3}\,\mathrm{ICC}\,\bar p(1-\bar p),$$ where $\mathrm{ICC}=\operatorname{Var}(p_x)/[\bar p(1-\bar p)]$ is the correlation between the outcomes of a pair of attempts at the same randomly drawn task. (c) Under the oracle check the limit is $\Pr(p_x>0)$, which equals $\bar p$ if and only if $\mathrm{ICC}=1$.

*Proof.* (a) $\mathbb E[a\pi]/\mathbb E[a]-\mathbb E[\pi]=(\mathbb E[a\pi]-\mathbb E[a]\mathbb E[\pi])/\mathbb E[a]$. (b) $\pi(p)=sp/[f+(s-f)p]$ is increasing and, for $s>f$, strictly concave, with $\pi''(p)=-2sf(s-f)/[f+(s-f)p]^3$; Jensen's inequality gives the upper bound and a Taylor expansion the approximation. With a common check $\mathbb E[a_x\pi_x]=s\bar p$ and $\mathbb E[a_x]=s\bar p+f(1-\bar p)$, so $P_{\text{pool}}=\pi(\bar p)$. For the lower bound, $\pi(p)-p=p(1-p)(s-f)/[sp+f(1-p)]\ge0$, with equality only at $p\in\{0,1\}$. (c) $1-(1-p_x)^n\to\mathbf 1\{p_x>0\}$, and $\mathrm{ICC}=1$ exactly when $p_x\in\{0,1\}$. $\square$

Part (a) matters to anyone who validates a check on a sample of outputs and then uses it to resample. Validation reports the pooled precision. Resampling shifts weight towards the tasks the generator finds hard, where acceptance is rarer and precision lower, so the realised ceiling is lower by the covariance term. Tasks on which the check rejects everything do not enter the pooled precision at all, yet they lower the limit. Part (b) bounds the ceiling by its values at the extremes of within-task correlation. Tasks of equal difficulty give the pooled formula; tasks that are always or never solved give single-attempt accuracy, and then no protocol beats one attempt. Between the extremes the intraclass correlation does not fix the ceiling. With $\bar p=0.5$, $s=0.9$ and $f=0.2$, a mixture of tasks with $p=0$ and $p=0.625$ has intraclass correlation 0.25 and ceiling 0.706, while an even mixture of $p=0.2$ and $p=0.8$ has the larger correlation 0.36 and the larger ceiling 0.738. What holds with a common check is an ordering by spread: if one distribution of task difficulty is a mean-preserving spread of another [@rothschild1970], its ceiling is no higher, because $\pi$ is concave and conditional Jensen's inequality applies to the added noise. In the Beta family of Figure 1c, with $\bar p=0.6$, $s=0.9$ and $f=0.2$, the computed ceiling falls from 0.871 at an intraclass correlation of 0 to 0.817 at 0.3, 0.703 at 0.7 and 0.6 at 1. Stroebl and colleagues capture the same mechanism with a mixture of easy and hard tasks and Bayesian updating after rejections [@stroebl2024, app. C], and Schaeffer and colleagues trace the aggregate scaling of the share of solved problems to the distribution of per-task success [@schaeffer2025]. Proposition 3 gives the exact gap and its bounds without assuming a mixture.

### 4.3 Checking the same candidate again

**Proposition 4.** Let $q(y)$ be the probability that an attempt produces candidate $y$, $\mathcal C$ the set of correct candidates, and $\beta_k(y)$ the probability that $y$ passes all of $k$ verdicts. If each candidate must pass $k$ verdicts, the ceiling is $$\pi^{(k)}=\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathcal C}q(y)\beta_k(y)}{\sum_y q(y)\beta_k(y)}.$$ (i) If verdicts are a deterministic function of the candidate, $\beta_k=\beta_1$ and $\pi^{(k)}=\pi$ for every $k$. (ii) If verdicts are independent given the candidate, with single-verdict acceptance probability $\alpha(y)=\beta_1(y)$, then $\beta_k=\alpha^k$ and, as $k\to\infty$, $\pi^{(k)}$ tends to the $q$-weighted share of correct candidates among those with the largest $\alpha$.

*Proof.* Apply Proposition 1 with candidate-level acceptance $\beta_k(y)$. In case (i) the $k$ verdicts coincide. In case (ii) terms with $\alpha(y)$ below the maximum vanish relative to the others as $k$ grows. $\square$

Re-checking thus reweights candidates by their probability of passing every verdict. It changes nothing when verdicts are fixed by the candidate. With independent verdicts it helps if the candidates the check accepts most often are correct and hurts if a wrong candidate is accepted more often than the right one, and between these extremes $\pi^{(k)}$ need not be monotone in $k$. Correlated but variable verdicts fall between cases (i) and (ii). This is a discrete analogue of over-optimising a proxy reward by best-of-$n$ selection [@gao2022overopt]. The same notation shows how $f$ should be measured. The rate that enters the ceiling is $f=\sum_{y\notin\mathcal C}q(y)\beta_1(y)/(1-p)$, which weights wrong candidates by how often the generator produces them. If a check tends to accept the generator's most frequent errors, its false-acceptance rate over attempts exceeds its rate over a list of distinct wrong answers.

### 4.4 A conditional reading of "power equals compute"

For an invoker whose check has likelihood ratio $s/f$, compute brings a task $x$ with $p_x<\rho$ into the attainable set at reliability $\rho$ if and only if $s/f$ exceeds the ratio of the odds of $\rho$ to the odds of $p_x$, and the price is $n^*(\rho)$ attempts. The invoker's verification competence sets the rate at which compute converts into reliability and caps the conversion. The competence need not be personal: a test suite, a formal verifier or a stronger model can supply the check. A purchased check must meet the same likelihood-ratio condition, and Proposition 3 adds that errors repeated across attempts, or shared by the generator and the check, lower what any check delivers. Whether compute can stand in for competence on a class of tasks becomes a question about the joint distribution of generator errors and check errors.

## 5. A measurement with a current model

### 5.1 Design

The items come from Omni-MATH, a benchmark of 4,428 olympiad-level problems with reference answers, released by its authors under the Apache-2.0 licence [@gao2024omni]. Eligible items were the HMMT February problems with Omni-MATH difficulty of at least 5.5, an integer reference answer and no reference to a figure; there were 69. The study used the first 40 in a seeded random order. Integer answers make correctness exact.

The generator was Claude Haiku 5.5 (`claude-haiku-5-5`), called through the `claude` command-line interface with low reasoning effort, no tools, a fresh session for every call and a fixed system prompt asking for a final answer and a stated confidence from 0 to 100. Each item received 6 independent attempts, 240 calls in all.

The checks were compared with the reference key. *Stated confidence* accepts an attempt whose own stated confidence is at least 90, a threshold fixed before the analysis. *Agreement* accepts a candidate if one further independent attempt gives the same answer; it costs a second generation and requires no competence. The *self-check* shows the same model, in a fresh session, the problem, the full proposed solution and its answer, and asks for a verdict. The *stronger-model check* puts the same request to Claude Sonnet 5.5 (`claude-sonnet-5-5`). The model checks were run once for each distinct pair of item and answer, using the first attempt that produced that answer; there were 56 such candidates. Each model check is therefore a deterministic function of the candidate in the analysis. This models an invoker who caches a verdict by final answer: a later attempt with the same answer but a different argument inherits the verdict given to the first. To see what the caching hides, both model checks were also run on each wrong attempt that repeated an answer already judged, shown with its own solution. HMMT problems and their answers are public, so the models may have seen them. Section 5.4 counts explicit appeals to memory in the outputs, and Section 5.5 repeats the measurement on harder items from other competitions.

The analysis of this set uses 352 calls. A pilot added 18 calls, and 2 one-word calls confirmed that a model identifier was available, for 372 calls. The revision added 22 verdicts on repeated wrong attempts and 248 calls for the robustness run of Section 5.5, for 642 calls in all. In the pilot, 10 attempts at Level-5 problems from the MATH-500 subset [@hendrycks2021; @lightman2023] were all correct, which led to the harder items; 8 pilot attempts at study items are excluded from the analysis.

The 3 items never solved under the original key were checked by hand, and the key is wrong for 1 of them. The problem defines $x_0=2009$ and $x_n=-(2009/n)\sum_{k<n}x_k$ and asks for $\sum_{n=0}^{2009}2^nx_n$. The reference solution unwinds the recursion as $(-1)^n\,2009\binom{2008}{n}$, but the recursion gives $x_1=-2009^2$ and hence $(-1)^n\,2009\binom{2009}{n}$, so the sum is $2009(1-2)^{2009}=-2009$, the model's answer on every attempt. Exact integer arithmetic in the test suite confirms the corrected value, and the analysis uses it. Results under the original key are reported below.

### 5.2 Estimation

For budgets of up to 6 generations, each protocol's reliability on an item is estimated by averaging its outcome over all orderings or subsets of the 6 observed attempts. This is the without-replacement construction of the unbiased estimator of the share of problems solved within $k$ samples [@chen2021], and it is unbiased for $n$ independent attempts given the realised verdicts. Beyond 6 generations the curves are plug-in values from each item's observed answer frequencies, with the vote computed by seeded simulation. These extrapolations treat an item never solved in 6 attempts as unsolvable, so the oracle values beyond 6 are lower bounds. Population values average over items. Intervals are 95% percentile intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of items; they treat the 40 items as a random sample from the eligible pool and attempts as independent within items. The intraclass correlation uses the one-way analysis-of-variance estimator for binary outcomes [@ridout1999]. For the agreement check, a budget of $n$ generations allows $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ pairs of a candidate and its confirming attempt. Up to 6 generations its reliability is averaged over all orderings of the observed attempts, with the first attempt returned when no pair agrees. Beyond 6 it is the closed form of Proposition 1 with $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ pairs, evaluated at unbiased estimates, from pairs of distinct attempts, of the probabilities that a candidate is correct and confirmed or wrong and confirmed.

### 5.3 The generator

Single-attempt accuracy was 0.879 (95% interval 0.800 to 0.954). Of the 40 items, 28 were solved on all 6 attempts, 2 were never solved and 10 were solved on some attempts (Figure 2a). The intraclass correlation of attempt outcomes was 0.51 (0.09 to 0.75). The 2 never-solved items account for 41% of the 29 wrong attempts. On one of them every attempt gave the same wrong answer with stated confidence between 85 and 90; on the other, stated confidence ranged from 5 to 15 and the attempts gave 3 different answers. No check can recover these items, so the oracle limit is 0.950.

### 5.4 The checks and what resampling delivered

The novice checks had likelihood ratios between 2.4 and 2.7 (Table 1; Figure 2b). Stated confidence was the weakest discriminator: it fell short of the threshold on many correct attempts, with sensitivity 0.57 (0.43 to 0.71), and the area under its curve of sensitivity against false acceptance across all thresholds was 0.76. Agreement had sensitivity 0.94 and false-acceptance rate 0.34. The self-check accepted every correct candidate, 38 of 38, and 4 of the 18 distinct wrong candidates. It accepted 3 of the 4 wrong candidates that were the most frequent wrong answer on their item and occurred more than once, and 1 of the other 14 (one-sided Fisher exact $p=0.019$). Because the wrong answers it accepted were mostly the frequent ones, its false-acceptance rate over attempts was 0.41 (0.05 to 0.73), against 0.22 over distinct wrong candidates. It shared the generator's systematic errors and caught most of its occasional ones. Earlier studies found that larger models evaluate the correctness of their own sampled answers with useful calibration on many tasks [@kadavath2022], that without external feedback they struggle to correct their own reasoning and sometimes make it worse [@huang2023], that self-critique fails on reasoning and planning tasks where a sound external verifier helps [@stechly2024], and that model evaluators recognise and favour their own generations [@panickssery2024]. The pattern here is narrower: the self-check rejected most one-off errors and accepted most repeated ones, which are the errors that resampling keeps producing. The stronger model accepted 1 of the 18 wrong candidates, for a false-acceptance rate over attempts of 0.07 (0.00 to 0.22) and a likelihood ratio of 14.5. Caching verdicts by answer moves the attempt-level rates more than the limits. When each of the 11 wrong attempts that repeated an already judged answer was judged on its own solution, the self-check agreed with its cached verdict on 9 and the stronger model on 10. With every wrong attempt judged on its own solution, the self-check's false-acceptance rate over attempts is 0.34 and its limit 0.920, and the stronger model's are 0.03 and 0.942.

The stronger model's advantage on these items rests partly on memory. A fixed pattern for explicit appeals to memory (recall, recollection, known answer, official answer, the competition's name, OEIS and similar terms) matches 19 of the 56 stronger-model verdicts, 7 of the 56 self-check verdicts and 12 of the 240 attempts. Because the pattern counts mentions, every matched verdict was read and classified, and the labels are frozen in the repository. Of the 19 stronger-model matches, 17 invoke a remembered answer to the problem, 1 recalls only a related bound and 1 says the model did not recall the answer; of the 7 self-check matches, 6 invoke a remembered answer. On the 18 wrong candidates, the stronger model invoked a remembered answer on 8 and rejected all of them, and it accepted 1 of the other 10. These counts are lower bounds on reliance on memory, because the pattern misses some phrasings, such as appeals to a known result, and only matched verdicts were read. Several of its rejections compare the proposed answer with a remembered published one. A check that recognises a problem and recalls its answer is not available on a new problem, so its false-acceptance rate on these public items probably understates its rate on unfamiliar tasks.

Every protocol starts at 0.879 (Figure 2c). The self-check reached 0.918 with 2 attempts and 0.920 with 3, after which further attempts changed nothing. The ceilings bind at the targets in Table 1. At a target of 0.90, the self-check, the stronger-model check and the oracle check reach it with 2 generations, the vote with 3 and agreement with 4; stated confidence and accepting the first output never do. At 0.95 only the oracle check reaches the target, with 5 generations, because every other limit lies below it. Counted item by item, the number of items whose own limit reaches 0.90 is 28 for accepting the first output, 31 for stated confidence, 35 for the self-check, 36 for the vote and for agreement, 37 for the stronger model and 38 for the oracle check. The differences between ceilings rest on few items. The oracle limit exceeds the self-check limit by 0.030 (0.000 to 0.073), a difference carried by 3 items, and exceeds the stronger-model limit by 0.0125 (0.000 to 0.038), carried by 1. The self-check limit exceeds single-attempt accuracy by 0.041 (0.017 to 0.066), and the oracle limit exceeds it by 0.071 (0.029 to 0.117). The measurement establishes that the self-check buys reliability over a single attempt and that, on these items, it and every other protocol except the oracle fall short of 0.95. It does not separate the self-check's shortfall against the oracle check from zero; with 40 items, that shortfall could be anywhere from nothing to about 7 points.

Proposition 3(a) concerns the abstaining limit, which Figure 2d shows next to the fallback limit. For the self-check, the stronger model and agreement the two coincide, because the only items on which these checks accept nothing are never solved. For stated confidence they differ: 13 items have no attempt with confidence of 90 or more, so the abstaining limit is 0.629, while fallback, which returns the first attempt on those items, reaches 0.892. Validated on these outputs, the self-check has pooled precision 0.946, but resampling with it reaches 0.920. The stronger-model check has pooled precision 0.991 and reaches 0.938. The covariance term reproduces both gaps, as an identity must. Most of the stronger check's gap comes from the 2 never-solved items: the check rejects their answers, so they never enter its pooled precision, yet they cost resampling 5 points. A Beta model of item heterogeneity at the measured intraclass correlation predicts the self-check's limit closely, at 0.920, but overstates the stronger check's, at 0.971, because the observed per-item success has an atom at zero that a Beta distribution with that correlation does not reproduce.

Under the original key, single-attempt accuracy is 0.854 with 3 never-solved items, and every limit is lower by 0.025. The measured false-acceptance rates rise to 0.51 for the self-check and 0.23 for the stronger model, because both accepted the correct answer that the key marked wrong. The key is itself a check, and its error would have been read as a failure of the model checks.

An attempt used 3,820 output tokens on average, 3,203 of them for reasoning, and cost $0.0019. A self-check verdict cost $0.0014 and a stronger-model verdict $0.0131, 6.7 times the price of an attempt. All 352 analysed calls cost $1.28.

### 5.5 A harder item set from other competitions

The robustness run repeats the measurement with the same model, prompts and protocols on Omni-MATH items from sources other than HMMT, with difficulty of at least 7, an integer reference answer and no reference to a figure. There were 115 such items; the run used the first 25 in a seeded random order, with 6 attempts each and one verdict per distinct candidate from each model check, 248 calls in all. The items come from international and national olympiads and team selection tests, which are also public. Their reference answers were not checked by hand.

Single-attempt accuracy was 0.580 (0.407 to 0.747) and the intraclass correlation 0.68 (0.46 to 0.84); 9 items were always solved and 7 never (Figure 3a). The self-check had sensitivity 0.98 and false-acceptance rate 0.44 (0.23 to 0.67); the stronger model 1.00 and 0.24 (0.00 to 0.48). Appeals to memory matched 33 of the 49 stronger-model verdicts, 25 of the 49 self-check verdicts and 36 of the 150 attempts; read one by one, 33 of the stronger-model verdicts and 21 of the self-check verdicts invoke a remembered answer.

With single-attempt accuracy of 0.58, the self-check's likelihood ratio of 2.2 gives a pooled precision of 0.752, but resampling with it reaches only 0.602, because errors concentrate on items that are rarely or never solved (Proposition 3(a)), and its gain over a single attempt is not resolved from zero. The self-check's curve even turns down after a few generations (Figure 3b). On 1 item it rejected the correct answer and accepted a wrong answer, so its precision there lies below the item's success rate, and by Proposition 1 resampling moves reliability down towards that precision. ### 5.6 What the measurement does not show

The measurement uses one generator at one effort setting, in a domain where answers are integers. It uses 40 items in the main set and 25 in the robustness set, so population intervals are wide, and only the main set's keys were checked by hand. Both sets are public competition problems, and the outputs show that the models recognise many of them. Apart from the re-judged repeated wrong attempts, which carried different solutions, each candidate received one verdict from each model check, so Proposition 4 is not tested and the repeatability of verdicts on the same candidate is unknown. The self-check saw the full proposed solution, which may make it more willing to agree with a fluent argument than a check given the answer alone. The stronger model comes from the same family as the generator, which may make shared errors more likely than for a check from another source. The main set was chosen after a pilot in which an easier benchmark produced no errors, so its regime is one in which the generator is usually right. On the robustness set, where errors concentrate on items that are rarely or never solved, the same novice check bought almost nothing, as Proposition 3(a) predicts.

## 6. Who holds the check

The propositions locate the input that binds. For a person who cannot verify, compute buys reliability up to the precision of whatever check they use, and beyond that point they must obtain better verification. Better verification has different owners depending on its kind. A stronger model used as a checker is frontier inference, and the compute behind it is detectable, excludable and quantifiable and is produced by an extremely concentrated supply chain [@sastry2024]; access to it and its price are set by its providers. A test suite, an execution sandbox or a proof checker instead runs on ordinary hardware, but it verifies only what someone has specified: the tests to run, the property to prove. With such tools the competence the invoker lacks moves into writing the specification. In the measurement, the stronger model recovered most of the gap that a perfect check would close on the main set and all of it on the harder set, where the self-check gained almost nothing, but on these public problems it invoked a remembered answer in at least 30% of its verdicts on the main set and 67% on the harder set (Sections 5.4 and 5.5). Part of its advantage is therefore memory, which a new problem would not offer.

The conditional version of "power equals compute" that survives says that compute converts into reliable outcomes at a rate set by verification and only up to the verification's precision. Verification can be bought as model inference under its providers' control, whose errors can coincide with the generator's, or built from tools that require someone to state precisely what counts as correct. The measurement leaves open how the ceilings behave in domains without exact answers, where the key itself is a fallible check; how repeatable model verdicts are, which decides whether re-verification can raise a ceiling; and what sensitivity and false-acceptance rates human novices achieve when they judge model outputs, which no study here could measure and which the consulting experiment suggests is well above zero for tasks outside a system's capability.
